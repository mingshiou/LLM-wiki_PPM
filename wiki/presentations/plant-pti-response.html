PAMP-Triggered Immunity (PTI)

The Foundational Cell-Surface Defense in Plants

Unlike vertebrates with specialized circulating white blood cells and antibodies, every individual plant cell possesses autonomous innate immune capacity to perceive and restrict microbial invaders.

Surface Surveillance → Phosphorylation Relay → Coordinated Defenses

1
Welcome students. Today we explore the molecular foundation of plant defense: PAMP-Triggered Immunity (PTI). A guiding question: Plants have no bloodstream, no wandering macrophages, and no adaptive antibodies. How does a single tomato leaf cell detect an invading bacterium within minutes of contact? The answer lies in plasma membrane pattern recognition receptors that stand continuous surveillance.
Conceptual Framework

The Multi-Layered Plant Immune Architecture

The Zig-Zag Framework: Conceptualizing self versus non-self recognition in plants

Layer 1: Cell-Surface Immunity (PTI)

Cellular Location: Outer plasma membrane & apoplast

Sensory Hardware: Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)

Molecular Targets: Conserved microbial signatures (PAMPs / MAMPs)

Defensive Scope: Broad-spectrum basal resistance; halts colonization by non-adapted microbes.

Layer 2: Intracellular NLR Immunity (ETI)

Cellular Location: Cytosol and host cell nucleus

Sensory Hardware: Nucleotide-binding LRR receptors (NLRs / R-genes)

Molecular Targets: Pathogen-specific virulence effectors (Avr factors)

Defensive Scope: Rapid, robust resistance; frequently culminates in hypersensitive cell death (HR).

The Evolutionary Dynamic: PTI represents the ancient, foundational defense line. Pathogens evolved Type III effectors to suppress PTI (causing susceptibility), which in turn provoked the evolution of intracellular NLR receptors (ETI).
Jones & Dangl (2006); Cook et al. (2015)
2
Walk students through the spatial separation: Layer 1 (PTI) acts at the exterior cell boundary, detecting shared microbial molecules that pathogens cannot easily mutate away. Layer 2 (ETI) acts inside the host cell, sensing specific sabotage proteins (effectors). PTI is the frontline wall preventing 99% of ambient microbes from causing disease.
Molecular Ligands

Conserved Microbial Signatures (PAMPs / MAMPs)

Indispensable structural and physiological components of microbial life

Bacterial Flagellin

Epitope: flg22

A 22-amino-acid peptide from the conserved N-terminus of bacterial flagellin (from Pseudomonas, Xanthomonas, Acidovorax).

Vital for flagellar motor assembly and motility in water films.

Elongation Factor Tu

Epitope: elf18

An 18-amino-acid epitope from bacterial EF-Tu, the ubiquitous GTPase driving ribosomal protein translation.

Highly expressed bacterial protein released during apoplastic cell lysis.

Fungal Chitin

Epitope: Chitin Oligomers

Linear β-(1,4)-linked N-acetylglucosamine polymers forming the structural scaffolding of fungal cell walls.

Completely absent in plant tissues; an unmistakable signature of fungal presence.

Why "MAMPs"? Because non-pathogenic, beneficial rhizobacteria (e.g. Bacillus and Pseudomonas) also carry flagellin and EF-Tu, the broader term MAMPs (Microbe-Associated Molecular Patterns) is commonly used.

Boller & Felix (2009); Chang et al. (2019)
3
Emphasize why PAMPs are evolutionary traps for pathogens: Flagellin is required for swimming, EF-Tu is required for translation, and chitin is required for fungal integrity. If a bacterium mutates flagellin to evade FLS2, it frequently loses motility and fails to infect plant stomata.
Receptor Complex Topology

Cell-Surface PRRs: Molecular Surveillance Antennas

The FLS2–flg22–BAK1 Heterodimerization Complex and Cytoplasmic BIK1 Activation

Molecular Components & Membrane Topology

  • FLS2 (Transmembrane LRR-RLK): Spans the plasma membrane. Features 28 extracellular LRRs that directly bind flg22, an alpha-helical TM domain, and an intracellular kinase.
  • BAK1 / SERK3 (Transmembrane Co-Receptor LRR-RLK): Also a transmembrane protein! Features 5 extracellular LRRs that sandwich the C-terminus of flg22, forming an active extracellular complex (Sun et al., 2013).
  • BIK1 (Receptor-Like Cytoplasmic Kinase - RLCK): Strictly intracellular! Lacks extracellular and TM domains; anchored to the inner plasma membrane. Phosphorylated by BAK1/FLS2, then dissociates to activate RBOHD.
Boller & Felix (2009); Sun et al. (2013) Science
4
Crucial conceptual point for students: FLS2 and BAK1 are BOTH transmembrane LRR-RLKs. Flg22 acts as a molecular glue: its N-terminus binds FLS2, while its C-terminus is bound by BAK1's 5 LRRs. In contrast, BIK1 is an RLCK (purely cytoplasmic, anchored to the inner plasma membrane). Upon complex activation, BIK1 is phosphorylated and dissociates to turn on RBOHD and MAPK cascades.
Signal Transduction

Intracellular Signaling Cascades (0–15 Minutes)

The biochemical relay converting extracellular perception into nuclear and apoplastic defense

Step 1 • 0–2 Min

PRR Activation

FLS2 binds ligand, recruits BAK1, and initiates transphosphorylation.

→
Step 2 • 1–3 Min

Ion Fluxes

Plasma membrane Ca²⁺ channels open; cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ surges within seconds.

→
Step 3 • 3–10 Min

Dual Kinase Relay

MAPK cascades (MPK3/6) and CDPKs amplify phosphorylation signals.

→
Step 4 • 5–15 Min

Defense Deployment

RBOHD produces ROS; WRKY transcription factors activate PR genes.

Early Biophysical Events (Seconds)

Opening of calcium-permeable channels triggers a rapid calcium wave, activating Calcium-Dependent Protein Kinases (CDPKs) and altering membrane potential.

Phosphorylation Cascades (Minutes)

The cytoplasmic kinase BIK1 phosphorylates the NADPH oxidase RBOHD at the plasma membrane, while MAPKs enter the nucleus to reprogram transcription.

Boller & Felix (2009); Jones & Dangl (2006)
5
Notice how spatial information is converted: Surface event (receptor binding) triggers rapid biophysical changes (calcium influx within seconds). Calcium and BIK1 trigger the membrane engine (RBOHD), while MAPKs carry the chemical message into the cell nucleus to switch on defense genes.
Defense Output I

Hallmark Defense 1: The Apoplastic ROS Burst

Rapid enzymatic generation of Reactive Oxygen Species to poison and restrict invaders

The Enzymatic Engine: RBOHD

The primary producer of defensive ROS is plasma membrane-bound NADPH Oxidase (Respiratory Burst Oxidase Homolog D, RBOHD).

Cytosolic NADPH + 2O₂ → NADP⁺ + H⁺ + 2O₂˙⁻ → H₂O₂

RBOHD transfers cytosolic electrons across the plasma membrane to apoplastic oxygen, producing superoxide radicals that rapidly dismutate into stable hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂).

Three Functions of Defensive ROS

  • Direct Antimicrobial Toxicity: H₂O₂ oxidizes bacterial lipids, proteins, and cell surfaces directly in the apoplast.
  • Cell Wall Cross-Linking: Serves as a vital substrate for apoplastic peroxidases to cross-link lignin and structural cell wall proteins.
  • Systemic Alarm Signaling: Diffuses rapidly to trigger defense gene expression in neighboring leaf cells.
Burst Kinetics: Detectable in leaf tissue within 2 to 5 minutes of PAMP perception, peaking at 15–25 minutes, and safely returning to basal levels within 45–60 minutes.
Torres (2010); Gupta et al. (2024)
6
Undergraduates often ask: Why produce ROS outside the cell? Because that is where the invading bacteria reside! If ROS were generated in the cytoplasm, it would damage plant chloroplasts and mitochondria. RBOHD directs the oxidative weapon into the extracellular apoplast.
Experimental Methodology

Laboratory Quantification of the Apoplastic ROS Burst

Chemiluminescent, fluorometric, and pharmacological assays for oxidative defense

Three Standard Assays

  • 1. HRP-Luminol Chemiluminescence:
    Leaf discs incubated with luminol and Horseradish Peroxidase. Emitted photons are recorded in real-time as Relative Light Units (RLU).
  • 2. H₂DCFDA Laser Confocal Microscopy:
    Permeable non-fluorescent probe is oxidized by cellular ROS into green-fluorescent DCF for in situ visualization.
  • 3. Pharmacological Inhibition (DPI):
    Diphenyleneiodonium specifically inhibits NADPH oxidases. Abolishes the burst, proving enzymatic origin.
Gupta et al. (2024); Chang et al. (2019)
7
Notice the natural bell-shaped kinetic profile: The burst begins around 3–5 minutes post-elicitation, reaches a smooth, rounded peak at 15–20 minutes, and gradually declines back to baseline by 45–60 minutes. Pre-treatment with DPI (the specific NADPH oxidase inhibitor) completely suppresses the burst, demonstrating plant enzymatic origin.
Defense Outputs II & III

Hallmark Defenses 2 & 3: Physical Barrier Reinforcement

Structural defense outputs physically arresting pathogen ingress

Callose Papillae Deposition

Within 4–12 hours, plant cells activate PMR4 / GSL5 to deposit dense β-(1,3)-glucan cushions beneath penetration sites. Stained with 0.01% Aniline Blue under UV.

Stomatal Immune Closure

Guard cell PRRs perceive PAMPs → triggers Nitric Oxide (NO) and Abscisic Acid (ABA) signaling → turgor loss closes foliar entry portals within 1 hour.

Luna et al. (2011); Boller & Felix (2009)
8
Contrast the timing and structural actions: Callose papillae (left) are dense beta-glucan structural mounds synthesized beneath penetration sites to halt mechanical ingress. Stomatal defense (right) acts within 1 hour: guard cells lose turgor via NO and ABA signaling to seal foliar entry portals, preventing bacterial entry into the apoplast.
Pathogen Counter-Attack

Pathogen Counter-Attack: Effector-Triggered Susceptibility

Adapted pathogens deploy Type III Secretion Systems (T3SS) to sabotage host PTI

The Principle of ETS

If PTI were impenetrable, plants would never succumb to disease. Adapted phytobacteria (e.g., Xanthomonas, Pseudomonas) evolved a specialized molecular syringe: the Type III Secretion System (T3SS).

They inject dozens of Effector proteins directly into the host cytoplasm to dismantle the PTI machinery from within, producing Effector-Triggered Susceptibility (ETS).

Molecular Targets of Bacterial Effectors

  • Targeting Co-Receptors: Effectors dephosphorylate or degrade BAK1, cutting off perception.
  • Silencing MAPK Signaling: Bacterial phosphatases inactivate MPK3/MPK6 to stop transcription signals.
  • Suppressing the ROS Burst: Effectors disrupt RBOHD phosphorylation to extinguish oxidative firepower.
  • Core Effector Example: AvrBs2 in Xanthomonas carries glycerophosphoryl diester phosphodiesterase activity essential for apoplastic fitness.
The Arms Race Escalation: When pathogens suppress PTI via effectors (ETS), plants evolve intracellular NLR receptors to intercept those effectors — triggering ETI (Layer 2).
Jones & Dangl (2006); Potnis et al. (2015); Osdaghi et al. (2021)
9
This illustrates the second step of the Zig-zag model: 1. Plant develops PTI. 2. Adapted pathogen evolves effectors to suppress PTI, causing ETS. 3. Plant evolves NLR receptors to detect effectors, initiating ETI.
Translational Plant Pathology

Agricultural Application: Defense Priming by Beneficial Microbes

Plant Growth-Promoting Rhizobacteria (PGPR) as natural trainers of plant innate immunity

What is Defense Priming?

Plants colonized by beneficial rhizobacteria (e.g. Bacillus velezensis) do not continuously waste metabolic energy producing defenses.

Instead, they enter a primed state: a sensitized physiological condition where receptors and signaling kinases are pre-assembled for hyper-accelerated defense upon actual pathogen challenge.

Landmark Watermelon Case Study

In watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) primed with Bacillus PMB04/05 against Bacterial Fruit Blotch (Chang et al., 2019):

2,733×
Hyper-Amplified ROS Surge: Primed plants unleashed up to 2,733-fold higher apoplastic ROS bursts within 1 hour of pathogen challenge compared to unprimed controls.

• Callose papillae deposition surged over 150-fold, completely arresting bacterial ingress.

Biocontrol Insight: Strains that strongly prime host PTI achieve over 95% disease protection in field trials, demonstrating that activating plant immunity is often more decisive than direct in vitro antibiotic inhibition.
Chang et al. (2019); Gupta et al. (2024)
10
Connect molecular theory to agricultural reality: Biocontrol isn't merely microbes spraying antibiotics at each other in the soil. Friendly microbes prime and train the host plant's own PTI machinery, enabling leaves to react with a 2,000-fold stronger burst when a real pathogen strikes.
Rhizosphere Biology

The Root Immune Paradox: Microenvironmental pH Quenching

Why don't plant roots constantly launch destructive immune bursts against friendly soil bacteria?

The Biological Dilemma

Soil contains billions of beneficial rhizobacteria displaying flagellin. If root cells triggered constant ROS bursts and callose papillae, root elongation would be paralyzed (The Growth-Defense Trade-Off).

The Mechanism (Yu et al., 2019): Beneficial Pseudomonas secrete D-gluconic acid via the pqqF pathway, actively acidifying the root boundary microenvironment to pH 3.5–3.7.

Physiological ROS Quenching

  • Plant apoplastic NADPH oxidase (RBOHD) requires neutral to slightly alkaline pH (~6.5–7.0) for optimal enzymatic ROS generation.
  • At pH 3.7, the flg22-elicited ROS burst is completely abolished.
  • This allows beneficial bacteria to colonize root surfaces without eliciting toxic autoimmunity, while distal leaves remain fully primed for defense!
Microenvironment Principle: Plant immunity is not a rigid digital switch; its activation threshold is continuously modulated by local chemical microenvironments (pH, organic acids, nutrients).
Yu et al. (2019)
11
This explains a major paradox for students: If flagellin triggers immune attacks, how can plants coexist with friendly soil bacteria? The bacteria lower the root boundary pH to 3.7, which chemically switches off RBOHD, preventing self-harm while keeping systemic defenses intact.
